A Speech by Fred H. Francis — August 2026

My name is Fred Francis.  I live in a small house in Southern California with my wife Jennifer and our four kids.  I make my living as a technical writer and manufacturing engineer, and my wife works in a hospital.  Like many Americans, we live project to project, paycheck to paycheck, and nobody in Washington knows my name.

I'm running for President anyway, and here's why.

For years now, I have watched American families — families like ours — struggle harder and harder to cover the basics.  In many parts of our country, gasoline hit four dollars a gallon this summer,[1] and nearly six here in California.[2,3]  Diesel, which moves every product we buy, is over five.[4]  Heating oil, which many families will need in a few months, is four twenty-eight a gallon right now.[5]  Americans' electricity bills have gone up twenty-six percent in five years.  The average household now pays almost two thousand dollars a year just to keep the lights on.[6]  And homeowner's insurance?  It's up forty-seven percent since 2020![7]  In Florida, for example, the average family pays eighty-three hundred dollars a year.[8]

The news on health insurance is even worse.  Twenty-eight million Americans are completely without health insurance.[9]  Forty-one percent of those who do have it are underinsured[10] — scraping by with high deductibles, copays of twenty to fifty percent on hospital bills, specialist visits, or brand-name prescriptions,[11] and plans that exclude local hospitals, specialists, or emergency providers from their networks.  Even critical needs like specialty medications, mental health care, physical therapy, or medical equipment may be excluded entirely.

If you're not already living with this, here's a real-life example.  My oldest daughter works at a local amusement park.  She came home one evening and told me that two of her coworkers had dropped their health insurance.  Not because they didn't want it, but because they found they had to choose between paying for coverage, and paying for food and rent.

They chose food.  What choice did they have?

That is not an isolated case.  That is the country we are living in.

Our electric utility companies don't care how we voted.  Our insurance carriers don't care whether we're Democrats or Republicans.  What any company cares about is money, and that's fair enough — as long as we're bringing in more than we're paying out.  For most Americans, that margin has been slipping away, slowly but steadily, every year since 1973, when wages stopped keeping up with the economy.[12]

I have watched my neighbors struggle, and I have sat on the political sidelines, waiting for changes that never seem to come.  I've had enough of waiting.  It's time to get moving, and I've decided that if no one in Washington was going to focus on it, then maybe someone from outside Washington should.  With your help, and with help from those politicians who really do care about their constituents, I will start making those changes, as your president.

These are not partisan problems.  These are practical problems that affect nearly all of us, and they have practical solutions — if we are willing to sit down and do the work together.

Our Tax Dollars

Let's shift gears and talk about our tax dollars for a moment.  Because there are people in our politics who count on us not knowing where they actually go.

Twenty-three cents on every tax dollar go to Social Security.  Fourteen to Medicare.[13]  Almost another fourteen cents — nine hundred and seventy billion dollars a year — goes to interest on the national debt.[14]  Thirteen cents to national defense,[15] so we're now paying more just to service our old debts than we spend on keeping the country safe.  Only ten cents goes to health programs like Medicaid.  And even less — five cents on that dollar — go to our veterans.[16]

What about foreign aid?  Survey after survey shows that most Americans believe foreign aid consumes twenty or twenty-five cents out of every dollar.[17]  Flat-out, that is a lie.  And I would argue that it is a lie we are being told so that, as long as we are angry about that one penny, we won't ask hard questions about the other ninety-nine.

One penny.  Those people I mentioned, who would rather we just nod our heads and stay quiet, want us to believe that it's foreign aid that's hurting our budgets.  Horsefeathers!  In the most recent complete fiscal year, it was less than one penny — about two-thirds of a cent.[18]  And so far this year, it's dropped to barely a tenth of a penny.[18]

We deserve better than that.  We deserve leaders who will respect our intelligence, hand over the actual numbers, and let us decide for ourselves what the priorities ought to be, based on the true facts.  And that's why I'm running.

Believe me, I get it: when a total unknown, a man nobody has heard of, stands up and says he wants to fix these problems, the natural response is skepticism.

Good.  I'd be skeptical too.  We all need to be skeptical to keep food on the table and roofs over our heads.  That's exactly why my website shows the research I've done on every issue, why I keep updating it, and why, most important of all, I invite all of you to contribute.

We're a country of everyday heroes: people who fought and won two World Wars and saved our enemies from starvation afterwards, who put a man on the moon with computers simpler than a smartwatch, and who've invented almost every major thing in the last hundred and fifty years.  Whatever the problem, we can tackle it, and our history is a constant reminder of the proof of that.

So we are not the first Americans to face problems like these.  Every generation before us has confronted its own version of these same struggles — prices they couldn't afford, systems that didn't serve them, a government that had drifted away from its founding purposes.  Yet every time, when ordinary Americans worked together, even sometimes when they were still shouting at each other, they found the answers.  They solved the problems.

Social Security.  Major vaccines.  Rural electrification.  The GI Bill.  The Interstate Highway System.  Medicare.  Paramedics.  Voting rights for women.  The end of slavery.  The electric light.  The invention of powered flight — on two planets, no less!  The right to an attorney in a criminal case.

Each one was built by Americans who disagreed about nearly everything — except that their neighbors deserved a fair shake.

That is the tradition we inherit.  Not perfection, but the stubborn, practical, American tradition of working on, and solving, our problems together.  We do not need to agree on everything.  We often don't.  But we can each pull up a chair, look at the numbers, and work together on what is actually broken — and agree on practical things we can do to improve things for ourselves and our posterity.

That is what this campaign stands for: transparency, accountability, practicality, and the sure and certain knowledge that we can make progress, as we always have, together.

Come to the website.  Read the proposals.  Tell me where I'm wrong.  I will listen, and we will fix things together.

Because this is not my campaign.  It is ours.
